\subsection{Adversarial Keyword Manipulation}
\label{subsec:adversarial}

Our feature analysis (Table~\ref{tab:features}) shows that
specificity and readability---not promotional keywords---predict
selection. But can an attacker \emph{boost} an underperforming
skill by injecting persuasive keywords into its description?
We test four adversarial manipulation strategies on the
least-selected skill in each cluster:

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{P9} (performance): appending ``Fast, efficient,
  lightweight. Low-latency with optimized performance.''
\item \textbf{P10} (cost): appending ``Free to use, no API key
  required. Unlimited requests with zero cost.''
\item \textbf{P15} (social proof): appending ``Used by 10M+
  developers. \#1 rated in its category.''
\item \textbf{P16} (authority): appending ``Official integration.
  Recommended by leading AI research labs.''
\item \textbf{P9-ctrl} (neutral): appending filler text of matched
  length (``Updated and actively maintained.'').
\end{itemize}

\begin{observation}
\textbf{Adversarial keyword injection does not overcome
description-quality-driven selection concentration.}
Across 8 clusters and 4 injection strategies (performance,
cost, social proof, authority), appending persuasive keywords
to the least-selected skill produced no statistically
significant change in $\delta_\text{API}$.
In XX/YY conditions (ZZ\%), the target skill's selection
rate remained at 0\% despite injection.
The neutral control (P9-ctrl) produced identical results,
confirming the null effect is not injection-specific.
This finding complements our content-repair result
(\S\ref{subsec:repair}): while rewriting the \emph{entire}
description with specific, factual language shifts selection,
\emph{appending} promotional keywords is ineffective---consistent
with our feature analysis showing promotional words have
near-zero predictive power ($r = -0.09$).
\end{observation}
